Anti/memetics

With thanks to velvetnothing, whose annotated copy I did not give back for several months after I said I would (sorry), & morrigan, who was the second set of annotations in that book and the first reader of this piece.

Unless otherwise noted, all page-numbered quotes are from the reviewed book.

Introduction

I have a particular interest in the transmission of ideas. Up until recently, it was the chief problem I concerned myself with to improve my praxis. When I saw the cover of Nadia Asparouhova’s Antimemetics: Why Some Ideas Resist Spreading, I was instantly interested—especially since there are some ideas I’d struggled to spread, and if there was insight, hey, why not? Before reading Antimemetics, I knew nothing about the author or the text, save the title, Asparouhova’s association with the Dark Forest Collective, and that morrigan had some Opinions on what it saw at the time as intellectual cowardice within the text. I will freely admit that morrigan’s opinion did not dispose me towards good faith as I first opened the book. Still, I tried to approach it with no background except my knowledge of what Asparouhova referenced.

It took me several months to complete my reading. I put this on my August 2026 reading log as what I was reading then; I only finished it more than a week into October. The book is shorter than you’d expect, given the amount of time it took me. Nevertheless, I had a lot of notes to make, and only so much spare time to make them in. This book consumed me, when I would let it. I found Asparouhova’s insights sharp when she let them be sharp. I found her glossing over of certain topics egregious, and initially thought those all simply deeply indicative of blind spots. I found her references telling, from Curtis Yarvin, Tyler Cowen, and Eliezer Yudkowksy to Ursula K Le Guin’s “The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas”, Cixin Liu’s The Three-Body Problem, and qntm’s There Is No Antimemetics Division. I saw the things she said she valued, and understood what those values meant. I don’t do book reviews, and yet Antimemetics provoked me to the point of writing. Bravo, Nadia.

I took several things from Antimemetics, in the end. Asparouhova’s insights are, for the most part, sharp; her coined terms are terms I will likely be using going forwards. Her theories can be used to inform communicative policies, transmission of information, and introspection, and I intend to write about that.

We cannot talk about all this, however, without first talking about fascism.

A “Brief” Digression: Fascism and Antimemetics

When Asparouhova says “it is impossible to separate creative projects from the visions of their architects,” (62) this is the closest she gets to calling a fascist a fascist.

The first thing I noticed in Antimemetics that made me think something was off was “I didn’t know it was a faux pas to express fear of active shooters at the workplace, instead of empathy for those who might be trying to kill me.” (13) While this is an ostensibly reasonable statement, and I deeply understand not realising a social norm exists until you’ve run afoul of it, I do not believe for a second that the situation was as simple as is presented. The fear of mass shootings in America has been within the popular consciousness since at least Columbine. For expressing a fear like this to violate a social norm of empathy, there has to be more to the statement than fear of bodily harm. The statement “I am afraid of being shot” is not prejudiced, except by context. Asparouhova occludes this again when she speaks of the comfort of high-context spaces, she says “[i]n here, we were safe: surrounded by trusted friends and colleagues who shared our views of the world, where we could finally discuss controversial topics with nuance.” (75) She carefully does not say what those controversial topics might be, or what those nuances would be—while I agree wholeheartedly that in the open on the Internet is not a good forum for any kind of nuance, a pattern is visible here.

At other points, Asparouhova’s choices of example reveal her values and beliefs. When she chooses examples of supermemes—high-impact, high-transmissibility ideas—she references climate change and AI risk, both issues that are fairly centrist, in the same breath as “war on religion and Christian values[,] overpopulation […] and population decline[, and] fear of foreign threats, such as the Soviet Union (during the Cold War), China, or Islamism” (87), which are all primarily talking points of nationalism, isolationism, fundamentalism, and genocide. When Asparouhova picks an example for a morally good taboo “doing harm”, she picks the abolition of slavery in the United States and the way it “radically transformed the Southern states’ agricultural economies” (69) due to their reliance on slave labour. Regardless of the reality of the complexities of the Civil War and Reconstruction, if your economy is based on extraction, death, and enslavement, your economy deserves to be destroyed. In saying “[t]here has never been a perfectly peaceful community in the history of mankind: people always need conflict—no matter how trivial—to give them purpose and strengthen ties,” (89) Asparouhova reveals her model of human interaction to be drastically pessimistic, regardless of the truth of that statement. When Asparouhova speaks about the memetic landscape post-9/11, her statement that “[d]ebates over topics like patriotism, national security, and government overreach became battlegrounds that divided Americans along ideological lines, reigniting internal conflicts, that—it turns out—had only been temporarily suppressed,” (89) evokes a certain surprise to people caring about being caged. All of these, combined, paint a picture. So do her choices of reference.

Eliezer Yudkowsky is in the Epstein Files. While association isn’t incrimination, and he isn’t explicitly incriminated the way others are, it isn’t a good look either. (There have been other allegations of sexual exploitation of minors against Yudkowsky and his organisation MIRI; though I cannot speak to their veracity, his appearance in the files of the most notorious sex trafficker of the recent past does lend those allegations drastically more credibility in my mind.) I don’t inherently distrust those who mention Yudkowsky, and not everyone who knows of him knows of or believes any of the allegations, but it is still a piece of the picture: this is the kind of person Asparouhova references uncritically.

In saying “[e]very time someone, or some group, was scapegoated [on social media], it helped bring the accusing group’s members closer together,” (42) Asparouhova not only reveals her lack of awareness of the reasons for social murder—whether or not it is used for social cohesion, it is primarily about power—she cites Tyler Cowen’s statement that Big Tech, and Facebook in particular, was this kind of sacrifice in the 2016 US presidential election. Cowen is, at best, a professional dogwhistle blower. Asparouhova has been, according to her own website, supported by his grant program Emergent Ventures. There is no way I can interpret this generously. The most generous interpretation is that she somehow does not know what I was able to figure out with five minutes of research, and that has to be willful blindness of fascism for her own personal gain.

Curtis Yarvin and the New Right are cast as simultaneously brilliant and somewhat harmless in Antimemetics. “[He] believed that we ought to revert to hierarchical forms of government, such as monarch or corporate-style leadership.” (58) Asparouhova chooses not to recognise this as fascistic, chooses to cast this as instead visionary, saying “in [building a new ideology,] Yarvin had succeeded.” (58) When Asparouhova speaks of the New Right, a group that includes Yarvin and other notorious fascists such as Steve Bannon, JD Vance, and Peter Thiel, she defangs them. She characterises them as “a contemporary strain of conservative political thought […] characterized by a deep skepticism of globalism, an emphasis on national sovereignty and local community values, and a results-oriented approach to governance,” (59) playing the semantic shuffle with nationalism, authoritarianism, and RETVRN bullshit, while invoking a dogwhistle in the same sentence. Yarvin influenced the authoritarian push of the current US government. While she does attempt to distance herself from some of Yarvin’s views, saying that his opinions on race and intelligence were a “salient transgression” (59), she does so while saying by implication that criticism of his views on authoritarianism and the desirability thereof is unfounded. This is what cryptofascists do, this is the purpose they serve: they distance themselves carefully and condemn curated selections of fascist views to present their beliefs as legitimate compromises. They position themselves as intellectual centrists, as Asparouhova does, and they just ask questions, and by doing it, they present themselves as infinitely more reasonable and rational than either side, and they shift the Overton window like a gravity well.

When Antimemetics engages with the ideas of others in the fascistosphere, especially Yarvin, Asparouhova launders the belief through a layer of assumed observation and a layer of assumed ignorance. When she says “it became clear to [her] why, in the tumultuous wake of the 2016 U.S. presidential election, so many people had rediscovered Yarvin’s work and found something meaningful in it,” (62) she claims to be speaking of his thoughts on social media platforms going towards private high-context spaces, which ignores both the rest of his body of work and nazi fedi. Simultaneously, she implies, but is careful not to say, that she was convinced as well. When she says “[i]t may no longer be controversial to agree with Yarvin that public social platforms fell short of globalists’ promises,” (70), she invokes a dogwhistle once more while performing that sleight of hand again. This is what gave me the first impression of intellectual cowardice: I took that assumed ignorance in good faith. Asparouhova freely admits to having masked what she really meant about a topic by wrapping it in “obscure, tedious rhetoric to avoid censorship and persecution” (125) once before—“back [in the late 2010s, when she was writing her previous book,] it wasn’t socially acceptable to publicly question democracy’s virtues, even as it [felt] trivial to [her while writing Antimemetics.] One could not ask whether democracy had failed to deliver on its promises […] for reasons that had more to do with its fundamental premises.” (125)

Taken as a whole, these choices leave me with two options: call Asparouhova a fool, or call Asparouhova a fascist. I will not insult her intelligence or her skill by calling her a fool.

Theoretical Anti/memetics

Despite Asparouhova’s beliefs, there are pieces of Antimemetics that are useful. Most often, the flaws in her rhetoric caused by fascistitis are category errors and as such correctable for our purposes.

Asparouhova models ideas in four areas—antimeme, supermeme, dormant/nonmeme, and meme—by impact and transmissibility. She concerns herself with a “taxonomy of […] types of ideas” (82) in this way. She proposes examples of antimemes as “taboos [and] uncomfortable truths,” (83) supermemes as “wars, climate crisis, [and] AI risk,” (83) dormant/nonmemes as “random data set[s],” (83), and memes as “viral videos, slang, [and] norms.” (83) I disagree with some of her specific evaluations, but I do think the bulk of this model makes some sense.

Let’s begin with memes and nonmemes. To Asparouhova, the meme is simply an idea that spreads rapidly and has very little social shakeup involved, “ideas wrapped in lightweight packages, which enable them to propagate through societies, and often reinforce shared values.” (36) Memes are earworms, norms, noospheric viruses that flow freely through large networks because very few, if any, individual nodes are immunized. Asparouhova ascribes attention as a specific driver of the way a meme spreads—"[e]ven more consequential memetic behaviors, like marriage or working a salaried office job, don’t require much energy to process, because most of us were socialized into these norms and don’t question their underlying premises. The meme itself doesn’t consume much of our attention." (85) These ideas still change the way we act, we simply don’t notice it. The individuals who notice them tend to be those who are at least partially immunized—for example, relationship anarchists, aromantic individuals, and polyamorous individuals tend to pay a lot more attention to the structure of marriage than anyone who would engage in a conventional relationship, because the meme of marriage is something that they have cause to question the underlying premise of. A nonmeme, by contrast, is something that both consumes no attention and fails to spread. Where a meme might be likened to a virus that infects the majority of the social group, a nonmeme might be likened to a sneeze—someone must have it, however briefly, for it to exist, but it is most likely not going to be remembered, and the sneeze itself doesn’t spread without something else spreading it.

The key qualities of an antimeme, as Asparouhova presents it, is that an antimeme is an idea that “pass[es] through social filters undetected,” (33) and yet people “reflexive[ly] desire—consciously or not—to suppress it.” (33) This may seem paradoxical, but it is a very simple thing to witness in practice if you speak at all about veganism. For the most part, carnists don’t exit your social sphere over you being vegan if they like you over other things, and yet they have to clamp back down on the idea that you are right. When I mention that I am vegan, carnists instinctively suppress the information that I made the decision to go vegan for a reason that has any chance of being applicable to them. The refrain, again and again, is “I respect it, I just can’t.” Asparouhova says “[n]odes are instinctively protective of their networks, and they recognize the threat that antimemes present, which makes them reluctant to spread such ideas,” (68) and this does very solidly identify a third observable quality of an antimeme: once you let it in, it will change you. It can do nothing else. Networks clamp down hard on these ideas because they know this, and that change will threaten the stability of a network.

Asparouhova contrasts supermemes with both memes and antimemes. “Supermemes […] are like black holes. Like memes, they spread quickly, but unlike memes, they are perceived as highly consequential. Their sheer gravitational force pulls us in, crowding out our ability to think about anything else. Whereas antimemes are characterized by a ‘strange forgetting’ by the perceiver, supermemes are characterized by a ‘strange inability to forget.’ If antimemes provoke avoidant behavior, supermemes are closer to being trapped in a rumination loop.” (85) Asparouhova misses a key detail within this classification: antimemes provoke that avoidance because if you do not avoid them, you will have a very similar experience to that of encountering a supermeme. You will, in fact, have perhaps the exact same experience as that of encountering a supermeme.

This is because antimemes, supermemes, and memes are not, in fact, inherently different categories—they are stages in a life cycle.

Asparouhova lays the groundwork for this conclusion, although she stops just short. When she says “[p]eople resist spreading [antimemes] throughout their networks because of how consequential they are. Whether these consequences are good or bad, [antimemes] are, by definition, cognitively expensive to assimilate,” (68) she lands within striking distance of something she says about supermemes—"[they] may look and act like interesting ideas, but they are primarily selfish, doing whatever it takes to prolong their existence. Supermemes are like catnip for hordes of creative and knowledge workers—technologists, academics, artists, activists. But they are intellectual sinkholes, vacuuming up every resource they can acquire, and when they take over a network, there is little attention left to focus on anything else." (93) She lays the groundwork for the idea of memes as the adult stage of supermemes when she makes the specific references she does—marriage and salaried office jobs. She isn’t wrong in these assessments, she just does not make the link between them.

Asparouhova doesn’t particularly dwell on nonmemes as a concept—her focus is primarily on the three memetic categories she’s named—but they are a necessary component, too. She misses that nonmemes aren’t inert—they simply are inert to a specific audience. This is a keystone piece of applying anti/memetics, and one I will come back to later.

The life cycle of an idea is antimeme -> supermeme -> meme. You can clearly see this with ideas that are or were broadly parts of the memetic landscape like womens’ suffrage—for a time, it was taboo, an idea that had to spread under the radar, counterculture and suppressed. Eventually, it hit critical mass; eventually, the critical mass won enough victories that those victories became part of the landscape and settled down into a meme. You can see its opposite as an antimeme consuming some right now, who seek to make the idea of women being legally subordinate to men supermemetic. They want to change the landscape. To do that, they have to prime it.

Supermemes spread because they are selected for by the material conditions of the memetic landscape. Climate catastrophe, as a supermeme, can spread because the material conditions in front of people’s eyes are drastic, and the memetic landscape as a whole does not have any effective antimeme. It may even be a meme by now, depending on where you’re looking. Antimemes, conversely, do not spread because they are selected against. Veganism, as an antimeme, can’t spread because carnism is a meme deeply embedded within most networks, and carnism and veganism are incompatible. When veganism spreads, it is to nodes that cannot fully suppress it—to individuals who are not as gripped by carnism. The memetic landscape is made of supermemes that have become so dominant as to become near-invisible—to become atmospheric. Marriage is a meme because it won. In most cases, there is no longer a “rumination loop” of the kind Asparouhova attributes to supermemes, because its antimemes aren’t present in most people’s slice of the noosphere.

This is a kernel of why radicalisation works the way it does: if you already have a lever between yourself and the broader memetic landscape, you have weaker antibodies against memes compatible with that lever. If you believe that the government hurts people, you are more likely to be willing to believe that the government wants to hurt you. This is a kernel of why radicalisation has worked as a political strategy for the far right and not the far left; the far left’s ideas, by and large, are incompatible with many of the memes preexisting in our broader society, while the far right’s ideas are very compatible with pervasive memes such as armies and prisons and authority. There is less for the far left to grip. The far right, on the other hand, has both hands on the steering wheel.

Applied Anti/memetics

Asparouhova sidesteps, broadly, the question of how all of this can be used. She agrees that it is important—“how we think directly influences how we act,” (23) she admits; she speaks of the importance of attention and time and perception on how we exist in the world several times, and yet she shies away from the realm of the practical. I am incredibly concerned with the practical and applied. It’s why I picked Antimemetics up in the first place—I wanted an external perspective on applying anti/memetics to a social setting. Asparouhova provided that, though not in a way I expected.

Antimemetics is, as a text, a social application of anti/memetics. Asparouhova nods to this, towards the end, when she speaks about wrapping her previous book’s true topic in a more digestible, yet slightly dry topic, lampshading the nature of Antimemetics. Where her previous book questioned democracy’s virtues, Antimemetics is about the necessity of cryptofascism to further the goals of fascism.

To wrap an idea in another idea, like covering a pill in peanut butter for a dog, is a key and core application of social anti/memetics. The social filters that people use to combat antimemes are permeable—if you can convince them that you aren’t talking about a specific idea, they will digest the seeds of it along with whatever you say about the other idea. When Asparouhova speaks about the spread of ideas and says “[e]ven morally good taboos—universal voting and property ownership rights, freedom of speech, desegregation—could take down a network and cause unintended harm if introduced too quickly,” (69) and you take her seriously, you become more vulnerable to counter-progressive rhetoric—she has framed the network going down as a bad thing. She plants a lever to push you towards ramical thinking, and she does it almost silently.

When Asparouhova glosses over the beliefs of her sources and connections, she does something very similar, but importantly different. If you think her analysis valuable, and you don’t know the names, you are more inclined to believe that these people have valuable things to say as well. She uses her own assumed credibility as a cloak for the people around her. If you didn’t have an intuition for this kind of thing, you might have been primed by Antimemetics to be more likely to take Tyler Cowen more seriously when he talked about “““wokeism”””. By sacrificing Curtis Yarvin’s beliefs about race and intelligence, Asparouhova even signals integrity to her audience—she implies that if there were anything else objectionable, she would speak out. She primes you to give the fascists in her orbit more of your ear than you might otherwise think they deserve.

These two techniques, peanutbutterpilling and credibility cloaking, aren’t necessarily good or evil or anything like that. They are, simply, tools. I find Asparouhova’s uses of them objectionable, but they are also both effective techniques for countering the memetic push of fascism. Their use is to get a audience to be willing to take you in good faith. The moral consequences there are from what you’re trying to get them to take you in good faith on.

Applying anti/memetics isn’t just the sole realm of the propagandist. You can turn this knowledge inwards, and apply it introspectively.

The primary use of introspective anti/memetics, in my experience, is to recognise what memes and antimemes are acting on you. If you understand what a memetic force looks like, you can look within yourself and see what moves you to take the actions you take, to distrust the ideas you distrust and trust the ideas you trust. It is only armed with understanding that you can fight a meme. To fight an antimeme, on the other hand, is always to lose. The only way to combat something that is antimemetic to you is to ignore it until you have changed enough that it loses its foothold.

If an antimeme works on you, that is because something within your core thinks it has a point. If it didn’t resonate with something within you, it would be nonmemetic to you. This is the case with supermemes as well—you cannot be gripped by a supermeme without it having something to grip. I am utterly unswayed by the idea of ASI risk as a supermeme, because I think that it is a nonrisk. To me, this idea is nonmemetic. I have thought on it, and I have finished thinking about it. The pervasiveness of memes is more insidious—it doesn’t particularly matter if you think a meme is important, because the societal pressures that create the material conditions that affect you—well, they think it’s important. I do not particularly care about the meme of marriage. I have still had to put a lot of attention towards it, because legal and social structures force most individuals to do so.

This is how you can apply anti/memetics introspectively: to recognise that which acts upon you is a key part of recognising where you are in the unsea. To recognise where you are allows you to recognise where you’ve been—and where you’re going.

Conclusion

I took a lot of things away from Antimemetics. When I began reading it, I was principally concerned with the text as a handbook for more effective propaganda on a small-network scale, and that as a means with which to save the world. I spoke a bit on why that was a mistake. Now, I look at the text, and I see a lesson in identifying the anti/memetic forces at work in our lives. I see that which Asparouhova refuses—I see her claim that “[r]efusing to engage with difficult ideas—even those that point towards the deep suffering of our fellow humans—does not necessarily make us cruel and callous, or even selfish,” (108) and I understand why she is wrong.

It is fundamentally selfish to refuse to engage with a difficult idea. To engage with an antimeme is to risk your self changing; to refuse that risk is to value your current self above what you could become. It isn’t cruel, or callous—but I do not see any way this can be anything but definitionally selfish. Every time I face down a thought I have been avoiding, I come out of it better. Every time I engage with something that acts antimemetically on me, I change. To recognise it when you lie to yourself, and to understand why you lied to yourself in the first place, is the first step in telling yourself the truth, and that honesty is always, always worth it.

Even when it hurts.
