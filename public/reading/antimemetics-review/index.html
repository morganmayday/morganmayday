Introduction

I have a particular interest in the transmission of ideas. Up until recently, it was the chief problem I concerned myself with to improve my praxis. When I saw the cover of Nadia Asparouhova’s Antimemetics: Why Some Ideas Resist Spreading, I was instantly interested—especially since there are some ideas I’d struggled to spread, and if there was insight, hey, why not? Before reading Antimemetics, I knew nothing about the author or the text, save the title, Asparouhova’s association with the Dark Forest Collective, and that morrigan had some Opinions on what it saw at the time as intellectual cowardice within the text. I will freely admit that morrigan’s opinion did not dispose me towards good faith as I first opened the book. Still, I tried to approach it with no background except my knowledge of what Asparouhova referenced.

It took me several months to complete my reading. I put this on my August 2026 reading log as what I was reading then; I only finished it more than a week into October. The book is shorter than you’d expect, given the amount of time it took me. Nevertheless, I had a lot of notes to make, and only so much spare time to make them in. This book consumed me, when I would let it. I found Asparouhova’s insights sharp when she let them be sharp. I found her glossing over of certain topics egregious, and initially thought those all simply deeply indicative of blind spots. I found her references telling, from Curtis Yarvin, Tyler Cowen, and Eliezer Yudkowksy to Ursula K Le Guin’s “The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas”, Cixin Liu’s The Three-Body Problem, and qntm’s There Is No Antimemetics Division. I saw the things she said she valued, and understood what those values meant. I don’t do book reviews, and yet Antimemetics provoked me to the point of writing. Bravo, Nadia.

I took several things from Antimemetics, in the end. Asparouhova’s insights are, for the most part, sharp; her coined terms are terms I will likely be using going forwards. Her theories can be used to inform communicative policies and transmission of information, and I intend to write about that.

We cannot talk about all this, however, without first talking about fascism.

A “Brief” Digression: Fascism and Antimemetics

When Asparouhova says “it is impossible to separate creative projects from the visions of their architects,” (62) this is the closest she gets to calling a fascist a fascist.

The first thing I noticed in Antimemetics that made me think something was off was “I didn’t know it was a faux pas to express fear of active shooters at the workplace, instead of empathy for those who might be trying to kill me.” (13) While this is an ostensibly reasonable statement, and I deeply understand not realising a social norm exists until you’ve run afoul of it, I do not believe for a second that the situation was as simple as is presented. The fear of mass shootings in America has been within the popular consciousness since at least Columbine. For expressing a fear like this to violate a social norm of empathy, there has to be more to the statement than fear of bodily harm. The statement “I am afraid of being shot” is not prejudiced, except by context. Asparouhova occludes this again when she speaks of the comfort of high-context spaces, she says “[i]n here, we were safe: surrounded by trusted friends and colleagues who shared our views of the world, where we could finally discuss controversial topics with nuance.” (75) She carefully does not say what those controversial topics might be, or what those nuances would be—while I agree wholeheartedly that in the open on the Internet is not a good forum for any kind of nuance, a pattern is visible here.

At other points, Asparouhova’s choices of example reveal her values and beliefs. When she chooses examples of supermemes—high-impact, high-transmissibility ideas—she references climate change and AI risk, both issues that are fairly centrist, in the same breath as “war on religion and Christian values[,] overpopulation […] and population decline[, and] fear of foreign threats, such as the Soviet Union (during the Cold War), China, or Islamism” (87), which are all primarily talking points of nationalism, isolationism, fundamentalism, and genocide. When Asparouhova picks an example for a morally good taboo “doing harm”, she picks the abolition of slavery in the United States and the way it “radically transformed the Southern states’ agricultural economies” (69) due to their reliance on slave labour. Regardless of the reality of the complexities of the Civil War and Reconstruction, if your economy is based on extraction, death, and enslavement, your economy deserves to be destroyed. In saying “[t]here has never been a perfectly peaceful community in the history of mankind: people always need conflict—no matter how trivial—to give them purpose and strengthen ties,” (89) Asparouhova reveals her model of human interaction to be drastically pessimistic, regardless of the truth of that statement. When Asparouhova speaks about the memetic landscape post-9/11, her statement that “[d]ebates over topics like patriotism, national security, and government overreach became battlegrounds that divided Americans along ideological lines, reigniting internal conflicts, that—it turns out—had only been temporarily suppressed,” (89) evokes a certain surprise to people caring about being caged. All of these, combined, paint a picture. So do her choices of reference.

In saying “[e]very time someone, or some group, was scapegoated [on social media], it helped bring the accusing group’s members closer together,” (42) Asparouhova not only reveals her lack of awareness of the reasons for social murder—whether or not it is used for social cohesion, it is primarily about power—she cites Tyler Cowen’s statement that Big Tech, and Facebook in particular, was this kind of sacrifice in the 2016 US presidential election. Cowen is, at best, a professional dogwhistle blower. Asparouhova has been, according to her own website, supported by his grant program Emergent Ventures.

Curtis Yarvin and the New Right are cast as simultaneously brilliant and somewhat harmless in Antimemetics. “[He] believed that we ought to revert to hierarchical forms of government, such as monarch or corporate-style leadership.” (58) Asparouhova chooses not to recognise this as fascistic, chooses to cast this as instead visionary, saying “in [building a new ideology,] Yarvin had succeeded.” (58) When Asparouhova speaks of the New Right, a group that includes Yarvin and other notorious fascists such as Steve Bannon, JD Vance, and Peter Thiel, she defangs them. She characterises them as “a contemporary strain of conservative political thought […] characterized by a deep skepticism of globalism, an emphasis on national sovereignty and local community values, and a results-oriented approach to governance,” (59) playing the semantic shuffle with nationalism, authoritarianism, and RETVRN bullshit, while invoking a dogwhistle in the same sentence. Yarvin influenced the authoritarian push of the current US government. While she does attempt to distance herself from some of Yarvin’s views, saying that his opinions on race and intelligence were a “salient transgression” (59), she does so while saying by implication that criticism of his views on authoritarianism and the desirability thereof is unfounded.

Eliezer Yudkowsky is in the Epstein Files. While association isn’t incrimination, it isn’t a good look either. (There have been other allegations of sexual exploitation of minors against Yudkowsky and his organisation MIRI; though I cannot speak to their veracity, his appearance in the files of the most notorious sex trafficker of the recent past does lend those allegations more credibility in my mind.)

When Antimemetics engages with the ideas of others in the fascistosphere, especially Yarvin, Asparouhova launders the belief through a layer of assumed observation and a layer of assumed ignorance. When she says “it became clear to [her] why, in the tumultuous wake of the 2016 U.S. presidential election, so many people had rediscovered Yarvin’s work and found something meaningful in it,” (62) she claims to be speaking of his thoughts on social media platforms going towards private high-context spaces, which ignores both the rest of his body of work and nazi fedi. Simultaneously, she implies, but is careful not to say, that she was convinced as well. When she says “[i]t may no longer be controversial to agree with Yarvin that public social platforms fell short of globalists’ promises,” (70), she invokes a dogwhistle once more while performing that sleight of hand again. This is what gave me the first impression of intellectual cowardice: I took that assumed ignorance in good faith. Asparouhova freely admits to having masked what she really meant about a topic by wrapping it in “obscure, tedious rhetoric to avoid censorship and persecution” (125) once before—“back [in the late 2010s, when she was writing her previous book,] it wasn’t socially acceptable to publicly question democracy’s virtues, even as it [felt] trivial to [her while writing Antimemetics.] One could not ask whether democracy had failed to deliver on its promises […] for reasons that had more to do with its fundamental premises.” (125)

Taken as a whole, these choices leave me with two options: call Asparouhova a fool, or call Asparouhova a fascist. I will not insult her intelligence or her skill by calling her a fool.
